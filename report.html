Executive summary

Cloud computing replaced a capital purchase with an API call. The consequence is measurable: worldwide enterprise spending on cloud infrastructure services reached $143.4 billion in a single quarter (Q2 2026), growing 43% year on year — the eleventh successive quarter of accelerating growth and the fastest rate in eight years. Three providers take two thirds of it. Amazon Web Services holds 28% of that market, ahead of Microsoft Azure at 20% and Google Cloud at 15%, though both competitors are growing faster than AWS and Google has taken three points of share in a year.

This report examines AWS as the representative provider. It explains how its global infrastructure is organised, dissects an EC2 virtual machine into its component parts, and then provisions one three different ways — web console, command line, and infrastructure as code — so the three approaches can be compared on the same task. It closes with the economics: on-demand list prices retrieved directly from AWS's pricing feed show that Arm-based Graviton4 instances undercut their Intel equivalents by roughly 11% at identical vCPU and memory, and that AWS's own published commitment and spot discount ceilings (up to 72% and up to 90% respectively) dwarf any instance-type optimisation. The recurring theme is that the hard problems in cloud computing are not technical provisioning problems — provisioning is one API call — but problems of architecture, security posture and cost discipline.

1. What cloud computing is

The working definition remains the US National Institute of Standards and Technology's: cloud computing is a model for enabling ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction. NIST names five essential characteristics, and a service is not a cloud service unless it has all five.

Table 1. The five NIST essential characteristics, and what each one means when you are the one paying the bill.
CharacteristicWhat it means in practice
On-demand self-serviceYou provision a server without a purchase order, a ticket, or a human on the other end. Section 5 does exactly this in about ninety seconds.
Broad network accessResources are reached over standard network protocols from any client. The VM in section 5 is addressed over HTTP and SSH, nothing proprietary.
Resource poolingYour VM is a slice of a much larger physical machine, multi-tenanted with other customers. This is why "instance type" is a contract about a slice, not about a box.
Rapid elasticityCapacity scales out and back in on demand, ideally automatically. This is what makes the spot market in section 7 possible.
Measured serviceUsage is metered and billed per unit consumed — per instance-second, per GB-month, per request. Metering is what converts an architecture decision into a line on an invoice.

1.1 Service models: who operates what

The three service models differ in exactly one thing: where the boundary of your responsibility sits. Figure 1 draws that boundary rather than describing it, because the whole distinction is a boundary.

1.2 Deployment models

  • Public cloud — infrastructure owned by the provider, shared across unrelated tenants. The default, and the subject of this report.
  • Private cloud — the same operating model on infrastructure dedicated to one organisation, whether self-hosted or hosted. Chosen for regulatory or latency reasons, rarely for cost.
  • Hybrid cloud — public and private joined so that data and workloads can move between them. AWS Outposts, Azure Arc and Google Anthos exist to serve this.
  • Multi-cloud — more than one public provider, deliberately. Buys negotiating leverage and reduces single-provider exposure; costs portability effort and cross-provider data transfer fees.

2.1 Why this report examines AWS

  1. It is the largest, so it is the most consequential to understand. At 28% of a $143bn quarter, more workloads run here than anywhere else.
  2. It is the oldest. EC2 launched in 2006. Its abstractions — instance, AMI, security group, availability zone — were copied by its competitors, so learning them transfers to Azure and Google Cloud almost directly (see Table 6).
  3. Its pricing is public and machine-readable. Every price in section 7 was retrieved from the same feed the AWS pricing page reads, which means the cost analysis here can be verified rather than believed.

The choice is not an endorsement. Section 10 compares the three head to head, and section 11 sets out what choosing AWS costs you.

3. AWS global infrastructure

Three nested concepts carry almost all the availability design in AWS, and they are the first thing to get right because every later decision inherits from them.

  • Region — a separate geographic area (us-east-1, eu-west-1, me-south-1) with its own pricing, its own service catalogue and its own failure domain. Regions are isolated from one another by design: data does not move between them unless you move it.
  • Availability Zone (AZ) — one or more discrete data centres inside a region, with independent power, cooling and networking, connected to sibling AZs by low-latency private links. An AZ is the unit of blast radius.
  • Edge location — a point of presence for CloudFront and Route 53, far more numerous than regions, holding cached content close to users.
The single most important architectural rule in this report A workload confined to one Availability Zone has the availability of one data centre. Spreading the same workload across two AZs in one region costs nothing extra in instance price and removes an entire class of outage. Figure 4 and Figure 7 both draw two AZs for this reason.

4. Anatomy of an EC2 virtual machine

"Launch an instance" is a request assembled from seven or eight independent choices. Almost every mistake in this exercise is one of those choices made carelessly, so it is worth taking the instance apart before building one.

4.1 Reading an instance type name

m8g.large is not arbitrary. Reading it left to right: m is the family (general purpose), 8 is the generation, g is the processor (AWS Graviton; i would be Intel, a AMD), and large is the size. Sizes double as you go up — large is 2 vCPU, xlarge is 4, 2xlarge is 8 — and, as the price table in section 7 shows, the price doubles with them almost exactly linearly. That linearity is the reason "scale up" and "scale out" cost roughly the same on paper, and why the choice between them is an availability decision rather than a cost one.

5.1 Path 1 — the AWS Management Console

The console is the right first path because it names every decision out loud. It is the wrong tenth path, because nothing you clicked is written down anywhere.

  1. Pick the region first, top-right of the console. Every subsequent screen is scoped to it, and an instance launched in the wrong region is invisible from the right one — the most common "where did my server go" incident.
  2. EC2 → Instances → Launch an instance.
  3. Name and tags. Set Name = cloud-project-web. Tags are how you find, group and bill things later; an untagged estate becomes unbillable within about a month.
  4. Application and OS image (AMI). Choose Amazon Linux 2023, then switch the architecture selector to 64-bit (Arm). This must match the processor in the instance type chosen next — an x86 AMI will simply not appear as launchable on a Graviton type.
  5. Instance type. Choose t4g.micro. The screen shows vCPU, memory and the on-demand price per hour; compare it against Table 2.
  6. Key pair. Create a new key pair, type ed25519, format .pem. The browser downloads the private key once. There is no second chance and no recovery: lose it and the only route back in is to rebuild the instance.
  7. Network settings → Edit. This is where marks are lost.
    • VPC: the default VPC is fine for this exercise.
    • Subnet: pick a specific one, and note which AZ it is in — this is where the instance physically lives.
    • Auto-assign public IP: Enable. Without it the instance has no route in from the internet.
    • Firewall (security group): create one with two rules — SSH (22) with source My IP, and HTTP (80) with source Anywhere. The console offers "Anywhere" for SSH as a one-click option. Do not take it.
  8. Configure storage. 20 GiB, gp3. Expand Advanced and tick Encrypted.
  9. Advanced details. Set Metadata version to V2 only (token required). Paste the contents of scripts/user-data.sh into the User data box at the bottom.
  10. Launch instance, then watch the Instances list: the state goes pending → running and the two status checks go from initialising to 2/2 passed. The instance is reachable at 2/2, not at running.

5.2 Path 2 — the AWS CLI

The full runnable script is scripts/create-ec2-cli.sh in this project, including the tear-down path. The launch call itself is the heart of it: 

aws ec2 run-instances \
  --region us-east-1 \
  --image-id "$AMI_ID" \
  --instance-type t4g.micro \
  --key-name cloud-project-cli-key \
  --security-group-ids "$SG_ID" \
  --user-data file://scripts/user-data.sh \
  --metadata-options "HttpTokens=required,HttpEndpoint=enabled,HttpPutResponseHopLimit=1" \
  --block-device-mappings '[{"DeviceName":"/dev/xvda","Ebs":{
        "VolumeSize":20,"VolumeType":"gp3","Encrypted":true,"DeleteOnTermination":true}}]' \
  --tag-specifications "ResourceType=instance,Tags=[{Key=Name,Value=cloud-project-cli}]"

Two details in that script are worth more than the launch call. The AMI is resolved at run time from AWS Systems Manager rather than hard-coded: 

AMI_ID=$(aws ssm get-parameters --region us-east-1 \
  --names /aws/service/ami-amazon-linux-latest/al2023-ami-kernel-default-arm64 \
  --query 'Parameters[0].Value' --output text)

and the security group is scoped to the machine running the script, computed rather than typed: 

MY_IP="$(curl -s https://checkip.amazonaws.com)/32"
aws ec2 authorize-security-group-ingress --group-id "$SG_ID" \
  --ip-permissions "IpProtocol=tcp,FromPort=22,ToPort=22,IpRanges=[{CidrIp=$MY_IP}]"
What the CLI path exposes Run create-ec2-cli.sh twice and you get two instances, two security groups and two bills. The script creates resources but does not track them: there is no record of what it made, so there is no reliable way to change or remove it later except by searching for tags. That gap is the entire argument for path 3.

5.3 Path 3 — infrastructure as code (Terraform)

The full configuration is terraform/main.tf, with inputs in variables.tf and results in outputs.tf. The core resource: 

resource "aws_instance" "vm" {
  ami                         = data.aws_ami.al2023_arm.id
  instance_type               = var.instance_type
  subnet_id                   = data.aws_subnets.default.ids[0]
  vpc_security_group_ids      = [aws_security_group.web.id]
  key_name                    = aws_key_pair.this.key_name
  associate_public_ip_address = true
  user_data                   = file("${path.module}/../scripts/user-data.sh")

  root_block_device {
    volume_size           = 20
    volume_type           = "gp3"
    encrypted             = true
    delete_on_termination = true
  }

  metadata_options {
    http_tokens = "required"     # IMDSv2 required
  }

  tags = { Name = var.name, Project = "cloud-computing-report" }
}

Run it:

ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -f ~/.ssh/cloud-project -C cloud-project
chmod 400 ~/.ssh/cloud-project

cd terraform
terraform init
terraform plan  -var="my_ip_cidr=$(curl -s https://checkip.amazonaws.com)/32"
terraform apply -var="my_ip_cidr=$(curl -s https://checkip.amazonaws.com)/32"
# ... demonstrate ...
terraform destroy -var="my_ip_cidr=$(curl -s https://checkip.amazonaws.com)/32"

Three properties fall out of this that neither of the other paths has. terraform plan shows what will change before anything changes. The state file makes the infrastructure diffable and reviewable like source code. And terraform destroy removes exactly what was created — which in a student account is the difference between a $6 exercise and a $60 one.

The configuration also refuses to be misused: variables.tf carries a validation block that rejects my_ip_cidr = "0.0.0.0/0" outright, so the most common security mistake in this assignment fails at plan time rather than silently succeeding.

chmod 400 ~/.ssh/cloud-project          # ssh refuses a world-readable key
ssh -i ~/.ssh/cloud-project ec2-user@<public-ip>

# on the instance — confirm the bootstrap actually ran
sudo systemctl status nginx
sudo cat /var/log/cloud-init-output.log  # where user-data failures show up

# confirm IMDSv2 is enforced: the v1 style call must fail
curl -s http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/instance-id     # -> 401 Unauthorized
TOKEN=$(curl -sX PUT http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 60")
curl -s -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN" \
  http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/instance-id           # -> i-0a1b2c...

Then open http://<public-ip>/ in a browser. The page served by scripts/user-data.sh prints the instance id, instance type and availability zone it is running in — which makes it a usable load-balancer demonstration later, because you can see which of the two instances answered.

5.5 The instance lifecycle, and what each state costs

The four mistakes that cost marks in this exercise
  1. SSH open to 0.0.0.0/0. A new public SSH port is found by automated scanners within minutes. Scope port 22 to a /32, or use AWS Systems Manager Session Manager and open no inbound port at all.
  2. Mishandled private key. It is downloaded exactly once, it must be chmod 400, and it must never be committed to a repository. There is no recovery path.
  3. IMDSv1 left enabled. With IMDSv1, one server-side request forgery bug in your web app is enough to read the instance's IAM credentials. IMDSv2's token requirement closes it. Set HttpTokens=required.
  4. Stopping instead of terminating. See Figure 6. Stopped storage still bills.

7.3 Costs that are not the instance

The instance-hour is the number students optimise and the number that surprises nobody. The charges that generate the surprises are elsewhere:

  • EBS storage — billed per GB-month whether the instance runs, is stopped, or was terminated leaving an orphan volume behind. The current gp3 rate is on AWS's EBS pricing page; it is not quoted here, because that page serves its prices from a template rather than from the machine-readable feed used for Table 5, and every number in this report is quoted from a source that was actually read.
  • Data transfer out — inbound is free; outbound to the internet is not, and cross-AZ traffic is charged in both directions. An architecture that chats across AZs pays for every message.
  • Idle public IPv4 addresses — AWS charges hourly for public IPv4 addresses, including allocated Elastic IPs that are not attached to anything.
  • Snapshots and AMIs — retained by default, forgotten by default, billed by default.
The cost hierarchy, cheapest action first
  1. Turn it off. A development instance running only office hours costs about a quarter of one running all week. Nothing else comes close to this.
  2. Right-size it. Use CloudWatch to find instances at 5% CPU and move them down the ladder.
  3. Commit. Once the baseline is genuinely steady, a Savings Plan takes up to 72% off it.
  4. Use Spot for interruptible work. Batch jobs, CI runners, and training checkpoints tolerate a two-minute eviction notice; they should not be paying list price.
  5. Then optimise the processor choice for the last 11%.

8. Use cases, matched to instance families

Each case below names the workload, the instance family that fits it, and — importantly — the purchase model, because as Figure 8 showed, that choice moves more money than the hardware choice.

8.1 Public web and application hosting

The canonical case, and the one built in section 5. Auto Scaling group of general-purpose instances behind an Application Load Balancer across two AZs, state pushed to RDS and S3 so instances stay disposable (Figure 7). Family: m8g or c8g for the app tier, t4g for small or intermittent sites. Purchase: Savings Plan for the always-on baseline, On-Demand for the scaled-out peak. Why cloud wins: the peak-to-trough ratio. A retailer sizing owned hardware for Black Friday pays for that capacity for 365 days a year.

8.2 Batch processing and scientific computing

Video transcoding, genome alignment, Monte Carlo simulation, nightly ETL. The work is interruptible and embarrassingly parallel, which makes it the ideal Spot workload. Family: c8g for CPU-bound work. Purchase: Spot, at up to 90% off, with checkpointing so a two-minute eviction costs minutes and not the whole run. Why cloud wins: five hundred instances for one hour costs the same as one instance for five hundred hours, and finishes the job today.

8.3 Machine learning training and inference

Training on p5 instances (8× NVIDIA H100 at $55.04/hour), serving on smaller g6 instances. This is the workload Synergy credits with the market's 43% growth — GenAI services grew 165% year on year in Q2 2026. Purchase: Spot or capacity reservations for training; on-demand or committed for serving. Why cloud wins: the alternative is a $40,000-a-month machine you must keep busy to justify. Renting turns a capital argument into a utilisation one.

8.4 Development, test and CI environments

Ephemeral by nature: a preview environment per pull request, torn down on merge. Family: t4g burstable — most of these boxes are idle most of the time, which is exactly what the burstable credit model is for. Purchase: On-Demand plus a scheduled shutdown; Spot for CI runners. Why cloud wins: the environment can be identical to production and cost nothing overnight — with the Terraform in this project, identical is literal, because it is the same file.

8.5 Migration, disaster recovery and business continuity

Lift-and-shift of existing servers, or a warm standby in a second region. Family: match the source machine at first, then right-size once real utilisation data exists — migrations that right-size on day one usually right-size wrongly. Purchase: On-Demand during migration, Savings Plan once steady. Why cloud wins: a DR site that costs a few dollars a month while idle, versus a second data centre that costs the same as the first whether or not it is ever used.

9. Security and the shared responsibility model

AWS states the division plainly: AWS is responsible for the security of the cloud — hardware, the hypervisor, physical facilities, the managed service software. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud. On EC2, as Figure 1 shows, that boundary sits high: guest OS patching, firewall rules, identity, encryption configuration and application code are all yours.

Identity

Never use the account root user for daily work; enable MFA on it and put it away. Give applications an IAM role attached to the instance, never a static access key baked into an AMI or a config file. The role delivers temporary rotating credentials through the metadata service, which is what makes IMDSv2 a security control and not a formality.

Network

Two independent mechanisms, and you want both. Security groups are stateful, allow-only firewalls attached to the network interface — there is no deny rule, absence is denial. Route tables decide whether a subnet can reach the internet at all. In Figure 4 the database is unreachable because of routing, not because of a firewall rule; if someone later misconfigures the security group, the routing still holds. Defence in depth means each layer failing independently.

Data

Encrypt EBS volumes at rest (one checkbox, no measurable performance cost, and it is set in terraform/main.tf). Terminate TLS at the load balancer with an ACM certificate. Back up with EBS snapshots or automated RDS backups, and — because a backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis, not a backup — restore one on purpose at least once.

Detection

CloudTrail records every API call made in the account, which is what turns "someone launched a $55/hour instance" from a mystery into a name and a timestamp. CloudWatch alarms on metrics; a billing alarm is the single highest-value alarm in a student account. AWS Config can flag a security group that opens port 22 to the world the moment it appears.

Why IMDSv2 gets its own paragraph The instance metadata service lives at the link-local address 169.254.169.254 and hands out the instance's IAM credentials to anything that asks from on the instance. Under IMDSv1 "asking" was a plain HTTP GET — so any server-side request forgery bug in a web application running on the instance could be pointed at that address and would return live cloud credentials. IMDSv2 requires a PUT to obtain a session token first, and sets a hop limit so the token cannot be fetched through a proxy or a container boundary. It is one line in terraform/main.tf (http_tokens = "required") and it closes an entire class of breach.

11. Limitations and risks

  • Vendor lock-in is real, and it is not mainly about VMs. An EC2 instance running Linux is portable — it is a Linux box. The lock-in accumulates in everything around it: IAM policies, S3 semantics, Lambda, DynamoDB, the managed services that make AWS worth using. The more of AWS you use well, the more expensive leaving becomes. That is a deliberate feature of the business model, not an accident.
  • Egress pricing shapes architecture. Data flows in free and out billed. This asymmetry quietly discourages multi-cloud designs and gives data gravity a direction: it is always cheaper to move compute to your data than data to your compute.
  • Cost is elastic in both directions. The same self-service that provisions a server in ninety seconds provisions a $40,000-a-month GPU cluster in ninety seconds. Without budget alarms, tagging and Config rules, an organisation finds out at the end of the month.
  • Concentration risk is systemic. Three providers hold 67% of public cloud. A regional AWS outage takes down a visible fraction of the internet with it, and no individual customer's architecture choices change that.
  • Sovereignty and compliance. Region choice determines legal jurisdiction over the data. For regulated workloads this is a legal question that precedes the architecture, not a technical one that follows it.
  • Capacity is finite. "Infinite capacity" is a marketing simplification. GPU instances in particular are subject to real capacity constraints and account quotas; InsufficientInstanceCapacity is a genuine error you will meet, and multi-AZ launch templates are part of the answer.

12. Conclusion

Provisioning a virtual machine on AWS is a small technical problem. Section 5 does it three ways, and the slowest of them takes about three minutes. That ease is precisely why the difficulty moves elsewhere: the open SSH port, the enabled IMDSv1, the instance stopped instead of terminated, the On-Demand price paid for a workload that has been steady for two years. Each of those is a decision made in seconds and paid for over months.

The evidence assembled here points the same way three times. In Figure 8, the commercial model moves cost by up to 72% while the processor choice in Figure 9 moves it by 11% — so the cheap decisions should be made first. In Figure 7, availability turns out to be a property of what surrounds the instance rather than of the instance itself. And in Table 4, the difference between the three provisioning paths is not speed but reviewability and reversibility. The version of this exercise that is worth learning is not "launch an EC2 instance". It is: write the instance down as code, scope its access to what it needs, know what state it is in and what that state bills, and destroy it when the demonstration is over.

Meanwhile the market this sits in is growing 43% a year and has doubled in eleven quarters. Whatever proportion of that growth generative AI ultimately justifies, the operational skills in this report — provisioning, network isolation, identity, and cost discipline — are the ones underneath all of it.
